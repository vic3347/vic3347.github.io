Far Right Extremism, Grievance, and Violence in the US

Why Existing Theories Fall Short

  • Individual response models (strain, rational choice): violence stems from frustration, deprivation, or cost-benefit calculation.
  • Social context models (social learning, social control, life-course): violence stems from peers, family, weakened bonds, and turning points.
  • Far-right extremists are mostly drawn from groups with relative social, political, and economic advantage, so deprivation alone does not explain them.
  • January 6 participants were, apart from race and gender, largely indistinguishable from typical Americans.

A Different Explanation: Group Position

  • Group position theory (Blumer 1958; Bobo 1999): grievance is a defense of status, not excelusively a response to loss.
  • Dominant-group members may see other groups' gains as a threat to their "rightful place," even when their own conditions have not worsened.
  • Relative deprivation (Stouffer; Pettigrew) supplies the comparison; laissez-faire racism supplies the language of fairness.
  • Thus, equality demands are reframed as persecution, and violence is seen as defense and restoration.

Summary

  • Far-right violence is better understood as defense of group position than as a product of individual deprivation.
  • Perpetrators experience violence as defense of an order they see as natural, just, and under threat.

Hypotheses

  • H1-H3 (strain, rational choice): grievance, marginalization, and network embeddedness predict violence across all ideologies.
  • H4 (social learning): radical peers, family, and online exposure drive violence and absorb the effect of grievance.
  • H5 (social control, life-course): bond erosion and life disruptions predict violence regardless of ideology.
  • H6-H9 (group position): grievance predicts violence among the far right but not far-left or jihadist actors; the effect is stronger for dominant groups, adds explanatory power beyond strain, and is amplified by life-course disruption.
